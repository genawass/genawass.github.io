The Map of CVPR 2026

June 2026

CVPR 2026 accepted 2,672 main-conference papers. That is far more than any one person can read — but it is small enough to see all at once. I embedded every paper from its title, keywords and abstract, projected the whole corpus down to two dimensions, and let the topics arrange themselves. What follows is a tour of that map and a few things it reveals about where computer vision actually is this year.

One dot per paper

Below is the entire conference. Each point is one accepted paper, placed so that papers about similar things sit near each other; colors are 24 topic clusters the data fell into on its own. Hover to read titles, click a cluster in the legend to isolate it, and zoom into any neighborhood you care about.

All 2,672 CVPR 2026 papers, embedded with a sentence-transformer and projected to 2D with UMAP. Color = automatically discovered topic cluster.

The first thing the map makes obvious is how generative and language-shaped the field has become. The big continents are diffusion-based image and video generation, vision-language models, and 3D reconstruction — and they are no longer separate islands. They bleed into each other, because the same backbones (diffusion transformers, CLIP-style encoders, large multimodal models) now show up everywhere.

What the conference is about

Collapsing the map into cluster sizes gives the topic landscape. The single largest cluster this year is feed-forward 3D reconstruction — the DUSt3R / VGGT lineage — with a little over 200 papers, followed by a stack of diffusion-for-generation clusters and a deep bench of vision-language understanding.

The 24 clusters, sized by paper count and labeled by their most distinctive terms (c-TF-IDF over each cluster's titles and keywords).

The ideas everyone is building on

Cutting a different way: how many papers even mention a given idea, anywhere in their title or abstract? This is the closest thing to a popularity contest for techniques.

Share of the 2,672 papers mentioning each idea (document frequency over title + abstract). A paper can count toward several.

A few things jump out:

  • Multimodal (23%), video (20%), and diffusion (20%) are now the water everyone swims in. Add vision-language models (18%) and LLMs (14%) and the generative-multimodal core is unmistakable.
  • Gaussian Splatting has lapped NeRF. 3DGS appears in 5.5% of papers; NeRF and radiance fields in just 1.2%. Two years ago that ratio was reversed.
  • Mamba / state-space models stayed niche at under 1%. The transformer (12% explicit, far more implicit) is still the default, despite the hype cycle around its successors.

How topics connect

Papers tag themselves with keywords, and those keywords co-occur in telling ways. Drawing the strongest 150 co-occurring pairs as a network shows the field's wiring: a dense generative-multimodal hub where image understanding, language modeling, CLIP and diffusion all interlock, with tighter satellite communities around 3D, video, and the transformer's internals (attention, KV-cache).

Top 150 co-occurring keyword pairs (connected groups only). Node size = how many papers use the keyword; color = community (greedy modularity); layout = force-directed.

How the field names its work

Finally, a lighter look — the linguistics of the titles themselves. Three out of four titles (74%) now use the Name: Description colon format. The dramatic "X is All You Need" meme has almost died out (just 2 titles), and so have question-titles (9). Papers like to open with Learning, Towards, Beyond, and Rethinking — and the most-reused acronyms (MLLM, VLM, LLM, VLA, LiDAR, GUI) are themselves a snapshot of the year's obsessions.

Title patterns, length distribution, opening words, and the most-reused method names across all 2,672 titles.

And the people

Behind the 2,672 papers are 12,924 distinct authors. The typical paper now carries six authors (mean 6.8); the largest single author list runs to forty. Computer vision has become, decisively, a team sport.

Method & caveats. This is a snapshot of the accepted main-conference papers only. Embeddings come from the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence-transformer, so proximity on the map means semantic similarity of text — not citations or impact. Clusters and the 2D layout are unsupervised, and cluster labels are auto-generated, so read them as helpful approximations rather than official categories. Keyword counts use each paper's own tags. With no prior-year data here, everything above describes CVPR 2026's composition, not multi-year trends. Charts are interactive (Plotly); the code lives on GitHub.
